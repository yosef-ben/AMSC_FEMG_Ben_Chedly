\section{Spreading of neurodegenerative diseases within the brain's connectome}

We have validated our finite element library for the solution of graph-based differential problems on a sequence of elliptic and parabolic benchmarks. We now consider a more application-oriented test case. In particular, we study the Fisher–Kolmogorov equation on a brain connectome's topology. This problem provides a natural extension of the previous linear parabolic models, as it combines diffusion along the graph with a nonlinear reaction term. At the same time, it provides a meaningful test for assessing the scalability of our framework through graph reordering and parallel programming techniques, with the particularly interesting perspective of modeling the propagation of infectious misfolded proteins through the brain, a process that is responsible for neurodegenerative diseases. The goal of this section is therefore not to provide a detailed biological study, but to investigate how the developed solver can be extended from controlled benchmark problems to a realistic network-based diffusion-reaction model.

\subsection{One-dimensional sensitivity analysis}
\label{sec:onedim}

Prion diseases are characterized by a chain reaction in which misfolded proteins force native ones into a similar pathogenic structure. A physics-based reaction-diffusion model can explain the spreading and growth of these proteins in neurodegenerative diseases: the Fisher-Kolmogorov equation combined with an anisotropic diffusion model \cite{weickenmeier2019prion,cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs,fornari2019prion}. Following the work described in \cite{weickenmeier2019prion}, we demonstrate the role of the individual model parameters through a one-dimensional sensitivity test, that we exploit also to validate the nonlinear solver before it touches a connectome. We solve

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} - d\,\frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2}
  - \alpha\,c\,(1-c) = 0,
\qquad (x,t) \in (-1,1) \times (0,20],
\label{eq:onedim}
\end{equation}
with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions,
\[
\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}(-1,t) = \frac{\partial c}{\partial x}(1,t) = 0 .
\]
The initial concentration is zero at every mesh node except at the central node, where $c(0,0)=0.1$. The interval is discretized using 200 linear finite elements. Backward Euler is employed with $\Delta t = 0.1$ and the nonlinear problem arising at each time step is solved by Newton's method; this is the fully implicit scheme. The sensitivity analysis combines the diffusion coefficients $d \in \{1.0, 2.0, 4.0\}\cdot10^{-4}$ with the reaction coefficients $\alpha \in \{1.0,2.0,4.0\}$. These are the choices made in~\cite{weickenmeier2019prion}, including the number of elements and the number of time steps.
\noindent
Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity} presents the results using an activation-time representation. For every pair $(x,c)$, the color denotes the first time at which the concentration at position $x$ reaches a prescribed level of $c$. The black curves are therefore contours of equal activation time.
Increasing $\alpha$ accelerates the local conversion of healthy proteins, whereas increasing $d$ enhances their spatial propagation. Consequently, both parameters increase the propagation speed, although through different mechanisms. The results are in agreement with the ones in \cite{weickenmeier2019prion}.

\noindent
From this sensitivity test we can also derive the main properties of prion-like diseases:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the progression of the disease is inevitable if there is an initial infectious seeding;
    \item the critical amount of misfolded proteins (quantity that defines the end of the incubation period) is reached at the seeding site as soon as the initial concentration and growth rate $\alpha$ are higher;
    \item the disease progression is characterized by a local increase at the initial seeding region and a subsequent global spread across the domain;
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The reference reports a preliminary study in which the time step was varied from $\Delta t/4$ to $4\Delta t$, using $\Delta t = 0.1$ as the reference value, with $\alpha = 2.0$ and $d = 2\cdot10^{-4}$. The authors concluded that $\Delta t = 0.1$ was sufficiently small to obtain a converged solution, whereas larger time steps produced a spurious increase in spreading. That study is described in words only: neither the data nor a figure is given, so the used criterion behind the statement cannot be reconstructed. What we can do is to repeat the experiment in order to observe the spurious solutions obtained for larger time steps.\\
We resort to the coordinate $x_{f}$ of the right front of the solutions as a diagnostic reference, defined as the point where the profile of the curve crosses $c = 0.5$. All the runs are compared at the common time $T = 19.2$, the largest time that is a whole multiple of every step tested: $\Delta t = \{0.025, 0.05, 0.1,0.2, 0.3,0.4\}$. Fig.~\ref{fig:timestep} shows the outcome of the experiment. The qualitative observation of the reference is confirmed. Increasing the time step moves the front outward and at a certain point, the front disappears altogether: for $\Delta t \ge 0.2$ the interval is essentially full, so no $c = 0.5$ crossing exists at all. Large time steps do produce a spurious increase in numerical propagation, exactly as described.\\
Quantitatively, the reading is more delicate. The three time steps that admit a front give:
\[
x_{f} = \{0.6889, \quad 0.7268, \quad 0.8145\}
\qquad\text{for}\qquad
\Delta t = \{0.025,\ 0.05,\ 0.1\},
\]
so the solution at $\Delta t = 0.1$ is not yet insensitive to further temporal refinement. This does not directly contradict the reference, since the criterion used there to assess convergence is not reported. Our numerical result only shows that, when the front position is used as a diagnostic, the solution still changes appreciably under further temporal refinement.

\subsection{The brain connectome as a metric graph}
\label{sec:connectome}

Having verified the nonlinear solver on a controlled one-dimensional problem, we now move to the network on which the disease is assumed to spread, reflecting the prion-like hypothesis that misfolded proteins propagate through the brain's axonal fiber tracts. Following \cite{fornari2019prion}, we represent the brain as a weighted undirected graph $\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})$ whose vertices are anatomical regions and whose edges are the axonal bundles connecting them. The graph is extracted from the public Budapest Reference Connectome v3.0 \cite{szalkai2017budapest}, which aggregates the tractography of $418$ healthy subjects of the Human Connectome Project. The downloaded fine-resolution graph contains $1015$ parcels and $71604$ connections. For each fine connection, the file records how many subjects exhibit that connection, together with aggregate statistics about its fiber count and length. As a first preprocessing step, we retain only fine connections occurring in at least five subjects. This reduces the number of edges from $71604$ to $37477$, while leaving all $1015$ parcels in the graph. The five-subject threshold is not explicitly stated in~\cite{fornari2019prion}; rather, it is inferred from the public dataset as the unique integer threshold reproducing the $37477$ fine edges reported in the reference. For comparison, thresholds of four and six subjects yield $40895$ and $34718$ edges, respectively.\\
The resulting fine graph is then mapped onto the $83$ anatomical regions of the FreeSurfer parcellation, as in~\cite{fornari2019prion}. Several fine parcels therefore collapse onto the same anatomical region. Among the $37477$ retained fine connections, $7895$ connect parcels belonging to the same final region and consequently become internal to an aggregated node. The remaining $29582$ connections join distinct regions, but many connect different fine parcels
belonging to the same pair of anatomical regions. Such parallel connections are merged, resulting in the final graph with $83$ vertices and $1130$ edges.\\
Each retained fine connection $f$ is assigned the weight $w_f =
\frac{n_f}{<\ell_f>}$, where $n_f$ and $<\ell_f>$ \footnote{<($\cdot$)> identifies an average over the fibers belonging to the same fine connection.} denote its statistically aggregated values of fiber count and length, respectively. When several fine connections join the same pair of anatomical regions $i$ and $j$, their contributions are summed:
\[
A_{ij}
=
\sum_{f\in\mathcal{E}_{ij}}
\frac{n_f}{<\ell_f>}.
\]
Thus, an edge of the $83$-region graph generally represents the aggregate contribution of several finer parcel-to-parcel connections, i.e. a bundle. Before any simulation is performed, the reconstructed connectome is verified against the graph statistics reported in~\cite{fornari2019prion}. The topology is recovered exactly and the weighted statistics agree with the published values. Our results are reported in Table \ref{tab:connectome}\footnote{An important point concerns the provenance of the quantities related to the fibers. Although \cite{fornari2019prion} describes the edge weights in terms of fiber quantities averaged over the cohort, the published graph statistics are reproduced by using the \emph{median} fiber-count and length fields of the public dataset. Specifically, the reconstruction uses $w_f = \frac{n_f^{\mathrm{median}}} {(<\ell_f>)^{\mathrm{median}}}$, followed by summation over parallel fine connections. In contrast, the corresponding mean fields do not reproduce the published values. We therefore
retain the median-based reconstruction, while treating this choice as a data-driven reconstruction of the published graph rather than as a statement about the undocumented implementation used in the reference.}.

\noindent
The definition of the connectivity weight has an important consequence for the resulting network. Since each fine-edge contribution is proportional to fibre count and inversely proportional to fibre length, connections supported by many short fibres receive larger weights, whereas long-range connections are penalized. After aggregation, this effect is inherited by the regional adjacency $A_{ij}$ through the sum of all fine connections joining the same pair of regions. At the level of the anatomical groups used later in this chapter, the total connectivity between the frontal and parietal groups is $174.8$ and that between the parietal and occipital groups is $189.7$. In contrast, the total frontal--temporal connectivity is only $0.85$, distributed over $14$ region-to-region connections, none of which exceeds $0.19$. The weighted network is therefore dominated by relatively strong short- and intermediate-range connections rather than by long-range bundles. Fig. \ref{fig:views}, \ref{fig:regions} and \ref{fig:connectogram} show the resulting computational domain.\\

\noindent
Fig.~\ref{fig:topology} summarizes the connectivity of the reconstructed
graph through the degree of its vertices, their weighted degree and the adjacency matrix. The latter reflects the same organization described in~\cite{fornari2019prion}. The two dense diagonal blocks correspond to connections within the two hemispheres, whereas the off-diagonal blocks are much sparser and contain the interhemispheric connections. There are strong connections also within the four lobes, indicated through the eight dashed boxes along the diagonal of the matrix (4 lobes $\times$ 2 hemispheres); each box bounds the longest consecutive run of its lobe in the node order, so one parietal and one temporal region per hemisphere, displaced by the interleaved limbic regions, remain outside and are located by the strips. The coloured strips along the four sides identify the seven anatomical groups used in~\cite{cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs}. Neither these groups nor the four cortical lobes are part of the Budapest dataset, whose vertices carry their FreeSurfer anatomical names, the hemisphere and a cortical or subcortical flag, but no anatomical grouping. Both partitions are obtained from those names through the name-based classification rules implemented in the solver, following for the cortex the standard Desikan--Killiany anatomy: the four lobes reproduce the assignment of~\cite{fornari2019prion}, while the seven groups reproduce the grouping through which Section~\ref{sec:corti} introduces the regional reaction coefficients. The non-zero adjacency values span $0.0085 \le A_{ij} \le 35.3221$, with a mean of $1.5702$, reproducing the published range and mean to the reported precision. The most important feature of panel~(c) is not the identity of a single strong or weak connection, but the pronounced heterogeneity of the network: most pairs of regions are either disconnected or only weakly connected, whereas a comparatively small number of connections carry much larger weights. Together with the heterogeneous weighted degree in panel~(b), this reveals the presence of strongly connected hubs embedded in a much more weakly connected network. This structure is directly relevant to the spread of the disease. In the network model of~\cite{fornari2019prion}, highly connected regions are expected
to become effective hubs for the propagation of misfolded proteins: the disease does not propagate over a uniform network, but through a small number of preferentially connected pathways and hubs.

\subsection{Network model and metric-graph formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

We solve the Fisher--Kolmogorov equation posed on the (metric) graph presented in the previous section;  on every edge $e$:
\begin{equation}
\partial_t c(s,t) - \partial_s\!\left[D_e\,\partial_s c(s,t)\right]
  = \alpha(s)\,c(s,t)\,[1-c(s,t)].
\label{eq:fk}
\end{equation}
Up to the nonlinear right-hand side, this is the same parabolic problem studied in the first part of this work. The only new ingredient is the nonlinear reaction term. The references solve a simpler system, with one unknown per node and no spatial structure inside the connections. We will refer to the models of the references \cite{cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs, fornari2019prion} as the \textit{nodal models}, that serve as the term of comparison next to our finite element solution. Each reproduced experiment uses the time scheme and way of treating the nonlinear term of its own reference. Table~\ref{tab:integrators} summarizes the two schemes.

\subsection{Comparison with the network model}
\label{sec:comparison}

We reproduce the baseline simulation of~\cite{fornari2019prion}: conversion rate $\alpha=0.5$, misfolded protein seeded in the entorhinal cortex at $c_0=0.1$, all other regions healthy and $100$ implicit steps of $\Delta t = 0.4$ years. Following the reference, the evolution is summarized by a biomarker: for a group of regions $\mathcal{I}$, the abnormality is the concentration averaged
over its vertices and expressed as a percentage,
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathcal{I}}(t)
  = \frac{100}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{I \in \mathcal{I}} c_I(t),
\label{eq:biomarker}
\end{equation}
evaluated for the four cortical lobes and for the network as a whole. The simulation is run both with the nodal model, the formulation of the reference implemented literally and with the finite element model, here at one element per connection, so that the two systems share the same $83$ unknowns and the same diffusion matrix and differ in the formulation alone; refined meshes enter with the sensitivity checks of the next section and the applications of Section~\ref{sec:corti}. The biomarker curves have the expected sigmoid shape in both. The $50$-percent crossing of the network average occurs at $11.11$ years for the nodal model and at $12.68$ years for the finite element model. The shift is expected: in the nodal model the domain consists of the $83$ vertices only, whereas in the finite element model the connections are part of the domain as well and they are initially uninfected. The same seed, $c_0 = 0.1$ on the two entorhinal vertices, must therefore also fill the metric space between the vertices before their concentrations rise and the finite element curve shifts to the right by about $1.5$ years. The two formulations describe different discrete systems, so their absolute crossing times are not expected to coincide. The activation sequence, however, is not reproduced by either: \cite{fornari2019prion} report a separation of roughly five years between the temporal and the occipital lobe; our four lobe curves cross the $50$-percent level within $3.9\cdot10^{-7}$ years of each other in the nodal model and $8.4\cdot10^{-3}$ years in the finite element model. The following section examines this discrepancy.

\subsection{What controls the regional separation}
\label{sec:damkohler}

Before explaining the discrepancy, it is useful to make some observations.\\
First, the graph. Although the reconstructed connectome reproduces the topology and global statistics reported in the reference, local differences in individual edge weights remain entirely possible, since part of the preprocessing pipeline had to be inferred from the public data rather than reproduced from a documented procedure. After validating our reconstruction against the published statistics, we retained this inferred preprocessing, which does not guarantee an entry-by-entry match with the adjacency matrix used in the reference.
\\
Second, the discrepancy is not caused by the numerical discretization (as seen in the previous section) or by the definition of the lobes. In particular, the near-synchronization is already present at the level of the $83$ individual regions: the biomarker concentrations all cross the $50\%$ level within $6.4\cdot10^{-5}$ years in the nodal model and within $0.20$ years in the finite element formulation, so no grouping of the nodes into lobes can separate the lobe curves by years. Nor is the reference's separation recovered by reasonable changes of the discretization rules: using the semi-implicit scheme in time instead of the fully implicit one, reducing the time step from $0.8$ to $0.05$ years and refining to eight spatial elements per connection leaves the four lobe crossing times within $0.04$ years of one another in every tested combination reported in the repository. These changes move the absolute timing of the curves, but not their near-synchronization.\\
Third, some implementation details remain unspecified: not only the complete graph-aggregation procedure, but also the precise implicit scheme adopted and the effective temporal scaling associated with the diffusion term. The reference defines the connectivity weights as
\[
A_{IJ}=\frac{n_{IJ}}{<l_{IJ}>},
\]
where the fibre count ($n_{IJ}$) is dimensionless and the fibre length ($l_{IJ}$) is measured in millimetres. Therefore, if these quantities are interpreted dimensionally,
\[
[A_{IJ}] = \mathrm{mm}^{-1}.
\]
On the other hand, the evolution equation is expressed in years, so that
\[
\left[\frac{dc}{dt}\right]=\mathrm{yr}^{-1}.
\]
The reaction term is dimensionally consistent because the conversion rate satisfies
\[
[\alpha]=\mathrm{yr}^{-1}.
\]
For the transport term, however, dimensional consistency would require an additional coefficient, say $\rho$, such that
\[
\frac{dc}{dt} +\rho Lc =
\alpha\,c(1-c),
\]
with
\[
[\rho]=\frac{\mathrm{mm}}{\mathrm{yr}},
\]
so that ($[\rho L]=\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$). In this interpretation, $\rho$ would set the temporal scale of transport on the network, analogously to how $\alpha$ sets the time scale of the reaction. Such a transport coefficient is not introduced explicitly in the published formulation. We therefore adopt the most literal interpretation of the reference and use the reconstructed connectivity weights directly, while treating their effective temporal scaling as a possible source of ambiguity. With the model and parameters reconstructed in this way, we do not recover the marked inter-lobe separation shown in the reference. This leaves the relative strength of network transport and local reaction as the main quantity to investigate. As shown below, changing this balance strongly affects the temporal separation between the lobes. This analysis therefore identifies the mechanism with which the discrepancy is observed, although the exact implementation used in the reference cannot be recovered without the original code and adjacency matrix.\\
To see what controls the separation, consider the two processes in the model separately and, since the answer will depend on the model, keep the two discrete systems side by side. With the reaction switched off, the nodal model and the finite element model read
\[
\frac{d\mathbf{c}}{dt} = -\rho\,L\,\mathbf{c}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\mathbf{M}\,\frac{d\mathbf{c}}{dt} = -\rho\,L\,\mathbf{c},
\]
where $L$ is the same matrix in the two, because at one element per connection the discrete Hamiltonian of Section 2.2 with $V = 0$ and diffusion coefficients $D_e = w_e$ coincides with the connectome Laplacian. What changes is the matrix in front of the time derivative. In the nodal model a vertex is a point and its mass is one. In the finite element model the value at a vertex multiplies the vertex basis function $\phi_v$ of Section 1.2, which is nonzero on every connection of the extended graph incident to the vertex ($e \in \mathcal{E}_v$): at one element per connection (the extended graph then coincides with the topological one) there are no interior nodes, so $\phi_v$ decreases linearly from one at the vertex to zero at the neighbouring regions. The sum of the elements of one row of $\mathbf{M}$ associated to vertex $v$ is $\int_\Gamma \phi_v\,\mathrm{d}s = \sum_{e\in \mathcal{E}_v} h_e/2$, with $h_e/2$ being half the length of every incident connection. With unit-length edges ($h_e = 1\,\,, \forall e \in \mathcal{E}$) we obtain for example $3$ at the frontal pole and $24$ at the caudate.\\
In both cases the solution is a sum of exponentially decaying modes. The mode with zero rate is the uniform state the network tends to; every other mode decays at the rate $\rho\lambda_k$, where the $\lambda_k$ are the eigenvalues of $L$ in the nodal model and the eigenvalues of $L$ relative to $\mathbf{M}$, $L\mathbf{v} = \lambda\,\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}$, in the finite element one. The slowest non-zero rate decides how long a difference between regions can survive, so diffusion homogenizes the network on the time scale
\[
\tau_{\mathrm{diffusion}} \sim \frac{1}{\rho\lambda_2},
\]
with $\lambda_2$ the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of the model at hand. Reaction alone makes the concentration grow at every vertex on the time scale
\[
\tau_{\mathrm{reaction}} \sim \frac{1}{\alpha}.
\]
\noindent
The two processes compete and their ratio defines the
Damk\"ohler number
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Da}
  = \frac{\tau_{\mathrm{diffusion}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{reaction}}}
  = \frac{\alpha}{\rho\,\lambda_2}.
\label{eq:damkohler}
\end{equation}
When $\mathrm{Da}\ll1$ the network homogenizes faster than the reaction grows and strong regional synchronization is expected; when $\mathrm{Da}$ is large the differences created by the seed survive and the lobes' abnormality curves can separate.\\
The two models evaluate \eqref{eq:damkohler} with different eigenvalues. For the reconstructed connectome the nodal model has $\lambda_2 = 0.7723$. In the finite element model, the same diffusive term $\rho L\mathbf{c}$ is filtered through the mass matrix $\mathbf{M}$, so the rate of change is governed by $\mathbf{M}^{-1}L$ rather than by $L$ alone. With mass lumping (see Section~\ref{sec:boundary} for the description of the mass lumping as a stabilization technique) the generalized spectrum changes again; in this connectome the second eigenvalue decreases from $0.0635$ (the value of the consistent mass) to $0.0606$, so the slowest diffusive mode becomes slightly slower. This decrease is not obvious a priori and has to be determined from the generalized eigenvalue problem. So, in the finite element model, the smallest non-zero eigenvalue is twelve to thirteen times smaller than $0.7723$. With $\rho=1$ and $\alpha=0.5$, the nodal model therefore has $\mathrm{Da}=0.65$, while the finite element model has $\mathrm{Da}=7.9$. At this point, however, the reasoning seems to break down: despite $\mathrm{Da}=7.9$, the lobe-wise curves remain nearly synchronized. This suggests that the slowest diffusive mode of the whole network is not necessarily the one that controls the separation between lobes.\\
For this reason, we also consider the eigenvectors associated with the spectrum containing $\lambda_2$. Each eigenvector describes a different spatial pattern of concentration differences that diffusion tends to smooth out, while the corresponding eigenvalue determines how fast that pattern decays. We find that the mode associated with $\lambda_2$ does not represent a contrast between anatomical lobes. Therefore, for the lobe-wise biomarker, the relevant quantity is not the slowest eigenvalue of the entire network, but the slowest relaxation rate among the modes that actually describe differences between lobes.\\
The eigenvector associated with $\lambda_2$ confirms this: its largest components are concentrated on the frontal and temporal poles and on the entorhinal cortices, with opposite signs in the two hemispheres. Therefore, the slowest diffusive mode of the whole network represents mainly an inter-hemispheric peripheral imbalance rather than a contrast between anatomical lobes. The rates of the lobe-scale modes follow from the compressed graph of Figure~\ref{fig:lobegraph}: for the nodal model they range from $10.0$ to $49.4$, the temporal-occipital contrast for instance relaxing at $21.6$, while for the finite element model the slowest one is $1.11$ with the consistent mass and $0.86$ with the lumped one. With these rates we can define a lobe-scale Damk\"ohler number,
\[
\mathrm{Da}_{\mathrm{lobe}} = \frac{\alpha}{\rho\,\lambda_{\mathrm{lobe}}},
\]
and at $\rho=1$ it is $0.05$ for the nodal model and $0.45$ and $0.58$ for the finite element model with the consistent and the diagonalized mass matrix: in every case we have a $\mathrm{Da}_{\mathrm{lobe}}<1$, so at the lobe-scale, transport still beats reaction and the synchronized curves are exactly what this number predicts. Figures \ref{fig:damkohler} and \ref{fig:lobegraph} quantitatively confirm the reading: in demonstrated runs the separation becomes appreciable as $\mathrm{Da}_{\mathrm{lobe}}$ crosses one and the separation of the reference, the grey band of panel (b) of Figure \ref{fig:lobegraph}, is reached by every model at $\mathrm{Da}_{\mathrm{lobe}}$ of order ten. The common $\mathrm{Da}$ of the figure, calculated with the nodal $\lambda_2$ so that a value of $\rho$ corresponds to a number, should therefore be read as a parameterization of the transport strength, while the scale at which each model separates its lobes is set by $\mathrm{Da}_{\mathrm{lobe}}$.

\noindent
With a uniform conversion rate, the reaction term cannot explain why different lobes cross the biomarker $50 \%$ threshold at different times, since the same value of $\alpha$ is used in every region. The ordering must therefore come from the connectivity of the network and from the position of the initial seed.\\
The temporal lobe contains the seed and is therefore activated first. From there, transport reaches the other lobes through the weighted connections of the connectome. At the level of total connectivity, the temporal lobe is strongly connected to the parietal lobe, with total weight $75.0$, and to the occipital lobe, with total weight $55.6$, whereas its direct connection to the frontal lobe is much weaker, with total weight $1.0$. This already explains why the frontal lobe is reached last: most of its activation occurs indirectly through other regions of the network, shown in grey in Figure \ref{fig:lobegraph}.\\
The total connectivity of a lobe, however, is not sufficient to determine its activation time. The biomarker \eqref{eq:biomarker} is an average over all the regions belonging to the considered lobe and these regions are not equally connected to the seed. What matters is therefore how the total connectivity is distributed among the individual regions. This explains, in particular, why the occipital lobe crosses the threshold before the parietal one even though its total connection to the temporal lobe is smaller. The temporal-occipital connectivity of $55.6$ is distributed over eight regions, corresponding to an average of $6.9$ per region and a median of $5.4$. The temporal-parietal connectivity is larger in total, $75.0$, but is distributed over twelve regions, giving an average of $6.2$ and a smaller median of $3.3$. Moreover, $49.4$ of the total parietal connectivity is concentrated on only the two inferior parietal cortices, while several other parietal regions are only weakly connected to the temporal lobe. These weakly connected regions cross the threshold later and therefore delay the average biomarker of the whole parietal lobe. This interpretation is supported by the regional data: within each lobe, regions that are more strongly connected to the temporal lobe tend to cross the threshold earlier. The weak-transport simulations then reproduce the expected ordering predicted by the structure of the graph. The two main conclusions therefore are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The relative strength of reaction and transport, quantified by the Damk\"ohler analysis, controls \emph{how much} the lobe curves separate in time.
    \item For a fixed seed and a uniform conversion rate, the spatial distribution of the connections of the reconstructed $83$-region connectome controls \emph{in which order} the lobes cross the biomarker threshold. A control on the fine graph of $1015$ parcels, kept in the repository, shows however that the intermediate ordering is sensitive to the coarse-graining, while the temporal lobe first and the frontal lobe late are not.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Mass lumping as a stabilization technique}
\label{sec:boundary}

With the finite element model, using one element per connection and the fully implicit time scheme, we observe that at low and moderate Damk\"ohler numbers the solution may temporarily leave the admissible interval $[0,1]$, but it eventually recovers. At $\mathrm{Da}=0.65$ the minimum concentration reaches only $-1.3\cdot10^{-4}$, while at $\mathrm{Da}=12.9$ it reaches $-0.08$ and the maximum reaches $1.02$. From the next tested scaling, $\mathrm{Da}=16.2$, the solution no longer recovers. At $\mathrm{Da}=129$ the concentrations reach $-0.41$ and $1.17$ by $t=15.6$ years, after which the nonlinear solver cannot complete the next time step. The same qualitative behaviour is observed with the semi-implicit scheme of Table~\ref{tab:integrators} and the failure persists under mesh refinement, indicating that it is not simply a coarse-mesh effect.\\
The origin of this behaviour can be understood from the finite element system
\[
\mathbf{M}\frac{\mathbf{c}^{n}-\mathbf{c}^{n-1}}{\Delta t}
+
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{c}^{n}
=
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{c}^{n}),
\]
where $\mathbf{H}$ is the discrete Hamiltonian of Section~2.2 with $V=0$ and diffusion coefficients $D_e=\rho w_e$, so that $\mathbf{H}=\rho L$ at one element per connection, while
\[
f_i(\mathbf{c})
=
\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}
\int_e
\alpha\,c_h(1-c_h)\,\phi_i\,\mathrm{d}s
\]
is the reaction vector. When transport is weak, the diffusion term becomes small and the update is approximately governed by
\[
\mathbf{c}^{n}-\mathbf{c}^{n-1}
\approx
\Delta t\,\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{c}^{n}).
\]
With the consistent mass matrix, $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ contains negative off-diagonal entries. This means that, through $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$, the reaction computed at one vertex can contribute with the opposite sign to the increment of neighbouring vertices, producing small negative concentrations. Once a concentration crosses below zero, the logistic term reinforces the error because
\[
c<0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
c(1-c)<0.
\]
The instability therefore develops in two stages: the consistent mass discretization first generates an undershoot and the nonlinear reaction then amplifies it. When transport is strong, diffusion smooths these small excursions and the solution returns to the physical interval; when transport becomes too weak, it can no longer compensate for them.\\
The Newton failure observed at $\mathrm{Da}=129$ is therefore a consequence rather than the cause of the instability. By $t=15.6$ years the solution has already reached $c_{\min}\simeq-0.41$ and the logistic reaction tends to drive the negative concentrations further down. Since the reaction is evaluated at the new time level, the fully implicit scheme requires the solution of a nonlinear system at every time step. Newton's method solves this system iteratively by correcting a current approximation until the residual is sufficiently small. In our implementation, the Newton iteration is damped and equipped with a safeguard that prevents the intermediate iterates from leaving the interval $[-0.5,1.5]$. At the step following $t=15.6$ years, the corrections required by the nonlinear system tend to push some concentrations below the lower bound. The damping can reduce these corrections, but eventually the solver cannot both remain inside the safeguard and reduce the residual to the required tolerance, so the iteration stops. The semi-implicit scheme, which solves a linear system at every step and has no such safeguard, continues at the same scaling and eventually diverges to $[-507,523]$. This confirms that Newton is not creating the instability: it is only the point at which the fully implicit computation can no longer follow an already nonphysical solution.\\
The same loss of positivity is reproduced in the one-dimensional problem of Section~\ref{sec:onedim}, showing that the mechanism is not specific to the connectome topology.\\
A standard remedy for this loss of positivity is \emph{mass lumping}. The consistent mass matrix is replaced by the diagonal matrix obtained from its row sums, while the reaction integral is evaluated using the vertex rule\footnote{The vertex rule approximates an integral over an edge $e=[i,j]$ of length $h_e$ using only the values at its endpoints. Applied to the reaction term,
\[
\int_e \alpha\,c_h(1-c_h)\phi_i\,\mathrm{d}s
\approx
\frac{h_e}{2}\left[
\alpha c_i(1-c_i)\phi_i(i)
+
\alpha c_j(1-c_j)\phi_i(j)
\right].
\]
Since $\phi_i(i)=1$ and $\phi_i(j)=0$, this reduces to
\[
\frac{h_e}{2}\alpha c_i(1-c_i).
\]
After summing over all edges incident to vertex $i$, one obtains
$f_i=M_{ii}^{L}\alpha c_i(1-c_i)$, so the reaction at vertex $i$ depends only on the local concentration $c_i$.
},
\[
f_i
=
M^L_{ii}\,\alpha_i c_i(1-c_i).
\]
Together, mass lumping and the vertex rule make the reaction treatment local: the lumped mass removes the cross-node coupling introduced by $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$, while the vertex rule makes $f_i$ depend only on the local concentration $c_i$. The reaction therefore takes the same node-wise form as in the nodal model, while the transport operator remains the finite element one.\\
We added mass lumping as an option in the finite element library and repeated the sweep under the same conditions. With the consistent mass, the concentration envelope already leaves $[0,1]$ at $\mathrm{Da}=12.9$ and the computation eventually breaks down at $\mathrm{Da}=129$. With the lumped mass, instead, all vertex concentrations remain within $[0,1]$ throughout the sweep, while the relevant physical behaviour is preserved: the lobe curves progressively separate as transport is weakened, as discussed in the previous paragraph.\\
At the original transport scale, the two formulations are almost indistinguishable, with the network crossing time changing only from $12.68$ to $12.72$ years. This shows that mass lumping stabilizes the finite element model in the weak-transport regime without significantly modifying the solution where the consistent formulation is already reliable. For this reason, in the rest of the chapter we use the consistent mass where transport is strong and the lumped mass where transport is weak, as in the seeding experiment of Section~\ref{sec:staging}. Every result from this point onward is therefore a finite element result.

\subsection{Seeding patterns and expected progression}
\label{sec:staging}

The two hallmark proteins of Alzheimer's disease enter the model through the initial spatial location alone. Tau inclusions appear first in the transentorhinal region and the expected progression is from the entorhinal cortex to the temporal lobe and then to the interconnected neocortex. Amyloid-$\beta$ deposits instead appear first in the neocortex and are expected to progress towards deeper structures, which are reached last \cite{weickenmeier2019prion}. On the graph, the two seedings are respectively the entorhinal vertices at $c_0=0.1$ and the $54$ neocortical vertices of the four cortical lobes, excluding the entorhinal and parahippocampal cortices, which belong to the allocortex.\\
Figure~\ref{fig:expected} shows the two computed progressions below their clinical counterparts, namely the staging drawings of~\cite{weickenmeier2019prion}. Three representative stages are selected as the first instants at which the network mean concentration reaches $10\%$, $40\%$ and $80\%$. The solution is the finite element one with lumped mass and the fully implicit scheme, at $\rho=0.005$, corresponding to $\mathrm{Da}=129$ and $\mathrm{Da}_{\mathrm{lobe}}=116$.\\
This transport scaling is essential for the progression to become visible. At $\rho=1$, the scale used in the references, transport is sufficiently fast to synchronize the network: in a control tau simulation the four lobes cross the $50\%$ level within $0.03$ years of each other and the three stages would appear almost spatially uniform. The choice $\rho=0.005$ should therefore be interpreted as a calibration of the transport time scale against the reference progression, rather than as an additional biological input. The only biological difference between the two simulations remains the location of the initial seed.\\
What each row of Figure \ref{fig:expected} recovers has to be stated carefully, because the first station of each expected pattern is prescribed by the seed rather than produced by the model and because every run of Eq. \eqref{eq:fk} with a positive reaction rate ends with the whole network at $c=1$. For the tau protein, the model produces one step of the expected sequence: from the entorhinal seed the pathology reaches the temporal lobe first, as the clinical description says. It does not produce the rest. The remaining lobes follow in the order of the graph-connectivity, so the occipital lobe, which the sequence of~\cite{fornari2019prion} leaves for last, is here the second to be involved and the frontal lobe, which the reference places immediately after the temporal one, is here the last, according to our Figure \ref{fig:damkohler}. In summary, the tau row starts from the entorhinal seed and fills the temporal lobe first; the spreading then moves posteriorly, the occipital lobe being the second of the four to cross the $50\%$ level, before progressively involving the rest of the network. The three displayed stages occur at $15.2$, $21.6$ and $27.6$ years, corresponding respectively to network mean concentrations of $10\%$, $40\%$, and $80\%$.\\
The amyloid-$\beta$ row starts instead from a diffuse neocortical seed and progresses towards deeper structures. The three displayed stages occur at $1.2$, $5.2$ and $12.0$ years. Averaging over each anatomical group the first time its regions cross $c=0.5$, the seeded neocortical regions cross first, at $4.4$ years, followed by the insula at $9.6$ years, the limbic regions at $11.7$ years, the remaining subcortical nuclei at $12.6$ years and finally the brainstem itself at $17.2$ years. This reproduces the downward progression described in the literature, from the neocortex towards the allocortex, deep nuclei and brainstem\footnote{The two staging comparisons do not carry the same evidential weight. In the amyloid-$\beta$ case, the diffuse neocortical seed already prescribes the first stage of the expected progression, so the resulting downward spread mainly verifies that the model propagates the pathology away from the seeded cortex. The tau case provides a more discriminating test, since after the temporal stage the model must determine which cortical regions are reached next and this is where the connectivity-driven ordering departs from the reference sequence}.\\
The same caveat discussed in Section~\ref{sec:damkohler} remains valid. Within the later tau stages, the relative ordering of the cortical lobes is determined by the connectivity of the reconstructed graph rather than by the clinical staging sequence. The four lobes cross the $50\%$ level in the order temporal, occipital, parietal and frontal, at $16.5$, $21.7$, $23.0$ and $25.9$ years, respectively, reproducing the ordering already observed in Figure~\ref{fig:damkohler}. The sequence of the four lobes is not itself part of the clinical staging, which is described by region: tau appears in the transentorhinal layer and the entorhinal cortex and spreads to the amygdala and the interconnected neocortex, while amyloid-$\beta$ appears in the neocortex and descends to the allocortex and the subcortical regions~\cite{weickenmeier2019prion}. It is this description that the rows of Figure~\ref{fig:expected} compare. The lobe sequence is instead a result of the network model of~\cite{fornari2019prion}, whose figure 7 reports temporal, frontal, parietal and occipital and describes it as agreeing with the observed staging. In our simulations, the frontal group is delayed by its weak effective connectivity, with the frontal pole being the least connected region of the graph. Since the conversion rate is uniform in the present model, regional differences in the progression are driven by connectivity alone. This motivates the introduction of region-dependent conversion rates, as in Section~\ref{sec:corti}, where additional biological heterogeneity is included in the reaction term.

\subsection{Deterministic model with regional reaction rates}
\label{sec:corti}

The previous sections showed what can be explained by the connectome alone. With a uniform conversion rate, the network connectivity determines how regional differences are transported and therefore strongly influences the ordering of the lobe-wise curves. However, this ordering does not fully reproduce the clinical one. We now introduce the biological heterogeneity that was absent from the previous model by allowing the conversion rate to vary between anatomical regions.\\
We follow~\cite{cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs}, who solve a patient-specific stochastic Fisher--Kolmogorov model on a $246$-node Brainnetome graph and estimate the reaction coefficient region by region from two PET acquisitions. Their formulation is nodal, with one unknown per vertex and a discrete graph Laplacian, whereas here we solve the corresponding deterministic problem with the metric-graph finite element model developed in this work. We do not attempt to reproduce the clinical trajectory of their patient. Instead, we transfer their estimated mean regional reaction rates to our $83$-region connectome and compare the resulting regional ordering. Making $\alpha$ region-dependent therefore introduces the first biological heterogeneity in the model beyond the connectivity itself.\\
The reaction coefficient is taken piecewise constant over seven anatomical groups, using the mean values reported in Table~1 of~\cite{cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs}:
\[
\alpha_{\mathrm{frontal}}=0.1801,\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{temporal}}=0.1421,\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{parietal}}=0.0627,
\]
\[
\alpha_{\mathrm{insular}}=0.1005,\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{limbic}}=0.1351,\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{occipital}}=0.0545,\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{subcortical}}=0.1147.
\]
These nodal values are interpolated linearly along each connection. The edge diffusivity is chosen as
\[
D_e=\frac{w_e}{\max_e w_e},
\]
since the absolute scaling of the connectivity weights is not specified in the reference. In the notation of Section~\ref{sec:damkohler}, this corresponds to
\[
\rho=\frac{1}{\max_e w_e}=0.0283.
\]
Using the vertex mean of the regional reaction rates,
\[
\overline{\alpha}=0.1252,
\]
gives the nominal value $\mathrm{Da}=5.72$. More importantly for the regional dynamics, the slowest lobe-scale relaxation rate of the finite element model with the consistent mass is, at this scaling,
\[
\rho\,\lambda_{\mathrm{lobe}}=0.031350\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1},
\]
which gives
\[
\mathrm{Da}_{\mathrm{lobe}}
= \frac{\overline{\alpha}}{\rho\,\lambda_{\mathrm{lobe}}}
= 4.0.
\]
This value was verified directly on the finite element meshes with one, four and eight elements per connection, for which the lobe-scale rate remains unchanged. The simulation therefore lies in a regime where transport alone already allows some regional separation, while the heterogeneous reaction rates can introduce an additional ordering. The time discretization is the semi-implicit scheme of Table~\ref{tab:integrators}, as in the reference, with the consistent mass matrix. We use $\Delta t=0.2$ years, four elements per connection, $3473$ degrees of freedom and a time horizon of $20$ years. Since the patient PET projection is unavailable, we use a synthetic initial condition: the two entorhinal vertices start from $c=0.10$, the same seed as every other experiment of this chapter, while the remaining regions are initialized at $c=0.01$, one tenth of the seed, to provide a small positive background in place of the unavailable PET field.\\
The absolute concentrations are not directly comparable with those of the reference, since we use a different connectome, a finite element formulation instead of the nodal model and a synthetic initial condition. What can be meaningfully compared is therefore the relative ordering of the anatomical groups.\\
Table~\ref{tab:corti} shows that the ordering agrees: we find the frontal group first and the occipital group last, using nothing but the seven reaction coefficients and the connectome\footnote{We notice that this agreement should not be interpreted as an ordering generated by the connectome alone. The ordering of the seven regional coefficients already fixes nineteen of the twenty-one pairwise orderings of the groups; the simulation therefore tests primarily whether transport and the initial condition preserve this imposed regional hierarchy, and they supply the remaining two pairs. Their contribution is real but secondary with respect to the heterogeneous reaction field in this experiment.}.

Figure~\ref{fig:expectedregional} repeats the two clinical seedings of that section, with the same scaling, the same lumped mass and the same three-stage rule, after replacing the uniform conversion rate by the seven regional rates above, rescaled to the same vertex mean $0.5$: the reaction budget, $\mathrm{Da}$ and $\mathrm{Da}_{\mathrm{lobe}}$ are unchanged and only the distribution of the rate differs, the rates running from $0.2177$ in the occipital group to $0.7195$ in the frontal one, and the horizon is extended to $80$ years for the slow groups. The tau lobes now cross the $50\%$ level at $14.7$, $19.9$, $29.7$ and $32.4$ years, in the order temporal, frontal, parietal and occipital, which is the clinical sequence the uniform rate could not produce, and the centroid of the regions above $c=0.5$ moves anteriorly across the three stages, from $+2$ to $+12$ mm, where the uniform run moves it posteriorly. The amyloid-$\beta$ row keeps its downward progression, with stages at $1.2$, $5.6$ and $13.2$ years. The comparison therefore suggests a division of roles rather than a strict separation: connectivity governs the transport pathways and contributes to the ordering, while the heterogeneous reaction field provides the dominant regional bias in this experiment.\\
A final remark concerns the provenance of the seven coefficients, which \cite{cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs} estimate from amyloid-$\beta$ acquisitions, while the sequence they restore in Figure~\ref{fig:expectedregional} is the tau one. What this experiment needed was not the conversion rate of tau, which no published calibration provides, but a regional heterogeneity of the reaction calibrated on patient data rather than assumed, and the estimation of~\cite{cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs} is, to our knowledge, the only such field available for this equation. It is obtained by solving an inverse problem through the model itself, which is also why an imaging rate of accumulation cannot be read directly as the coefficient $\alpha$ of~\eqref{eq:fk}: it is the observed change of a burden that transport, local conversion and the concentration already present produce together. The conclusion, moreover, does not rest on the particular values. Assigning the seven coefficients to the seven groups in all $5040$ possible ways at the same vertex mean, the clinical sequence never appears when the frontal coefficient does not exceed the occipital one and appears in $75$ percent of the assignments in which it exceeds it by a factor of three or more; a synthetic field, linear in the anterior-posterior coordinate and sharing with the calibrated one only that ratio, recovers the same sequence. The only property the result uses is therefore that the anterior groups convert faster than the posterior ones. Whether this holds for tau is not established, since longitudinal tau imaging reports a stage-dependent regional pattern rather than a fixed gradient~\cite{jack2018longitudinal,krishnadas2023rates}; what~\cite{cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs} demonstrates is the method by which an image-calibrated heterogeneity enters the model, and repeating their estimation on longitudinal tau acquisitions would provide conversion rates for tau itself, closing the comparison with the staging of Section~\ref{sec:staging} on the protein that staging describes. The connectivity carries the propagation and a regional heterogeneity of the reaction carries the order.

metric-graph formulation at the same $83$ unknowns takes $0.083$ s for its

order-of-magnitude check rather than a comparison, but it confirms that the P1
metric-graph formulation carries no cost penalty over the nodal network model
at the coarsest mesh, while allowing arbitrary refinement inside the
connections.
