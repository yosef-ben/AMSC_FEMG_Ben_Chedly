\subsection{The brain connectome as a metric graph}

Having verified the nonlinear solver on a controlled one-dimensional problem,
we now move to the network on which the disease actually spreads. Following
\cite{fornari2019}, we represent the brain as a weighted undirected graph
$\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})$ whose vertices are anatomical regions
and whose edges are the axonal bundles connecting them. The graph is extracted
from the public Budapest Reference Connectome v3.0, which aggregates the
tractography of $418$ healthy subjects of the Human Connectome Project. We
retain the connections occurring in at least five subjects and aggregate the
$1015$ fine parcels into the $83$ regions of the FreeSurfer parcellation,
removing the connections internal to a parcel.

The reconstruction is verified against the values published in
\cite{fornari2019} before any simulation is run. Table~\ref{tab:connectome}
collects the comparison. The topology is recovered exactly, and the weighted
quantities agree to within a few percent. The residual discrepancy is expected
and its origin is known: the downloadable file provides the median consensus
over the subjects, whereas the published statistics are averages over the $418$
brains, and the original $83\times 83$ averaged matrix is not distributed. We
also downloaded and aggregated the mean-mode graph with the same threshold; it
gives a mean adjacency of $2.4398$ and a mean weighted degree of $66.4339$,
both farther from the published values than the median reconstruction, so the
median graph is retained.

\subsection{Network model and metric-graph formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

On the connectome, \cite{fornari2019} solve a system of ordinary differential
equations with one unknown per region,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}c_I}{\mathrm{d}t}
  = -\sum_{J=1}^{N} L_{IJ}\,c_J + \alpha\,c_I\,[1-c_I],
  \qquad I = 1,\dots,N,
\label{eq:nodal}
\end{equation}
where $L = D - A$ is the connectivity-weighted graph Laplacian. This is a
nodal model: the brain regions are points and the connections carry no
internal structure.

The formulation developed in this work replaces the graph by a metric graph.
Every connection becomes an interval of unit length carrying its own diffusion
coefficient, the concentration is a continuous piecewise linear function on the
whole network, and the semi-discrete problem is
\begin{equation}
M\,\dot{\mathbf{C}} + K_D\,\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{C}),
\qquad
R_i(\mathbf{C}) = \int_{\Gamma} \alpha\,c_h\,[1-c_h]\,\varphi_i \,,
\label{eq:fem}
\end{equation}
with continuity of the concentration and the weighted Kirchhoff flux condition
imposed naturally by the weak formulation. The reaction vector and its Jacobian
are integrated with three-point Gauss--Legendre quadrature on every cell, which
is exact for the degree-four integrands generated by a P1 concentration and a
P1 reaction coefficient; the reaction is therefore a consistent finite element
integral and not a nodal Hadamard product.

Two differences between \eqref{eq:nodal} and \eqref{eq:fem} are worth stating
explicitly, because they account for essentially all the discrepancy observed
below. First, the mass matrix $M$ is not the identity, so a value prescribed at
a vertex has a metric support that depends on how many connections meet there.
Second, one element per connection makes the vertex unknowns of \eqref{eq:fem}
coincide in number with the unknowns of \eqref{eq:nodal}, but not in meaning,
and refining the mesh inside the connections adds degrees of freedom that the
nodal model cannot represent at all.

\subsection{Comparison with the network model}

We reproduce the baseline simulation of \cite{fornari2019}: conversion rate
$\alpha=0.5$, misfolded protein seeded in the entorhinal cortex at $c_0=0.1$,
all other regions healthy, and $100$ implicit steps of $\Delta t = 0.4$ years.
The biomarker abnormality of a region group is the concentration averaged over
its vertices, expressed as a percentage.

Both solutions remain in the physical interval, up to a transient undershoot of
order $10^{-4}$ in the finite element solution which we trace below to the
coarse mesh, and both approach the stable state $c=1$. The biomarker curves
have the expected sigmoid shape, and the $50$-percent crossing of the network
average occurs at $11.11$ years for the nodal model and $12.68$ years for the
finite element model, the difference being the metric support effect described
above.

The activation sequence, however, is not reproduced. \cite{fornari2019} report
a separation of roughly five years between the temporal and the occipital
lobe; our four lobe curves cross the $50$-percent level within
$2.9\cdot10^{-7}$ years of each other in the nodal model and
$5.7\cdot10^{-3}$ years in the finite element model. Since our nodal model is
\eqref{eq:nodal} implemented literally, the discrepancy cannot be a defect of
the discretization, and we devote the next subsection to explaining it.

\subsubsection{Convergence}

Before doing so we verify that the finite element solution is converged. The
spatial study keeps $\Delta t = 0.4$ and refines each connection into $1$, $2$,
$4$ and $8$ elements. The maximum deviation of the biomarker curves from the
finest run decreases as
\[
0.4389\%, \quad 0.1178\%, \quad 0.0229\%, \quad 0,
\]
over $83$, $1213$, $3473$ and $7993$ degrees of freedom. The small negative
transient disappears from two elements per connection onwards, confirming that
it is a coarse-mesh artefact and not a failure of the nonlinear solver. The
temporal study uses eight elements per connection and the steps
$\Delta t = 0.8, 0.4, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05$; successive differences between biomarker
curves give the observed rates $1.0552$, $1.0263$ and $1.0126$, converging to
the first order expected of the backward Euler scheme.

We retain $\Delta t = 0.4$ in the comparison above in order to match the
discretization of the reference, but we note that the study just described
moves the network crossing time from $11.50$ years at $\Delta t = 0.8$ to
$13.70$ years at $\Delta t = 0.05$. The absolute crossing times therefore carry
a time-discretization error of order one year, in our simulation as well as in
the reference, which uses the same step and a first-order scheme.

\subsection{What controls the regional separation}

Consider \eqref{eq:nodal} with the Laplacian scaled by a positive factor
$\rho$, so that $\rho=1$ recovers the published model. Linearising around a
small uniform concentration, the average over the network grows at the reaction
rate $\alpha$, while any imbalance between regions relaxes at the rate
$\rho\lambda_2$, where $\lambda_2$ is the Fiedler value of $L$, its smallest
non-zero eigenvalue. A difference between lobes can therefore survive long
enough to be visible only if the reaction is faster than the homogenisation of
the graph, that is if the Damk\"ohler number
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Da} = \frac{\alpha}{\rho\,\lambda_2}
\label{eq:damkohler}
\end{equation}
is large. For the reconstructed connectome we compute $\lambda_2 = 0.7954$ and
$\lambda_{\max} = 154.04$.

At the parameters of the reference, $\rho = 1$ and $\alpha = 0.5$, we obtain
$\mathrm{Da} = 0.63$. The connectome homogenises faster than the reaction
develops, and coincident lobe curves are the correct solution of
\eqref{eq:nodal}, not a numerical artefact. Figure~\ref{fig:damkohler}
quantifies this by sweeping $\rho$: the lobe separation grows from
$2.9\cdot10^{-7}$ years at $\mathrm{Da}=0.63$ to $6.92$ years at
$\mathrm{Da}=629$, seven decades of variation with the topology, the seed and
the conversion rate held fixed. Recovering the separation reported in
\cite{fornari2019} requires $\mathrm{Da}$ of order $10^{2}$, that is $\rho$
close to $0.005$. We conclude that the published figure cannot be produced by
the connectivity-weighted Laplacian at unit scale, whatever reconstruction of
the weights is adopted.

This also reconciles two results of this chapter that would otherwise look
contradictory. The deterministic model of Section~\ref{sec:corti} normalises
the diffusivity as $D_e = w_e/\max_e w_e$, that is $\rho = 0.0271$, and uses
regional reaction rates whose average over the $83$ vertices is $0.1252$; its
Damk\"ohler number is $5.79$, on the other side of the crossover, which is why
its regional curves separate clearly while those of the present section do not.

\subsubsection{A validity boundary for the discretization}

The same sweep bounds the finite element formulation. Above $\mathrm{Da}$ of
about $13$, that is $\rho \le 0.02$ at $\alpha=0.5$, the discrete concentration
leaves $[0,1]$ and then diverges: at $\rho = 0.005$ the nodal reference stays
within $[0.99987, 1]$ while the finite element solution reaches
$[-922, 308]$. The failure occurs identically with backward Euler and with the
semi-implicit Crank--Nicolson scheme, so it is not a failure of the Newton
solver, and it persists at $1$, $2$, $4$ and $8$ elements per connection, so it
is not a matter of resolving the front.

The cause is the consistent mass matrix. Its off-diagonal entries are positive,
so the semi-discrete system does not satisfy a discrete maximum principle;
while diffusion dominates, the diffusion matrix compensates, and once the
reaction dominates nothing does. It is worth observing that the nodal model
\eqref{eq:nodal} and the Hadamard product used by \cite{corti2023} are exactly
the mass-lumped counterparts of \eqref{eq:fem}, which is why they remain
bounded at every scaling tested. Introducing an optional lumped mass matrix is
the natural way to extend the present formulation into the reaction-dominated
regime, and we leave it as the immediate continuation of this work. Both
simulations reported in this chapter satisfy $\mathrm{Da}\le 6$ and are
therefore well inside the region where the discretization is reliable.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the conversion rate}

We repeat the conversion-rate experiment of \cite{fornari2019} with
$\alpha = 0, 0.1, \dots, 0.5$ over $80$ years. The qualitative conclusion of
the reference is recovered: lowering $\alpha$ delays the transition but does
not create an intermediate stable state, and every positive conversion rate
drives the network to $c=1$. The $50$-percent crossing of the network average
moves from $11.11$ years at $\alpha = 0.5$ to $59.33$ years at $\alpha = 0.1$
in the nodal model, and from $12.68$ to $67.81$ years in the finite element
model. For $\alpha = 0$ the reaction vanishes and the two conserved quantities
are preserved to round-off: the nodal average drifts by $3.9\cdot10^{-16}$ and
the normalised finite element metric integral $\mathbf{1}^{\!\top} M
\mathbf{C}/\mathbf{1}^{\!\top} M \mathbf{1}$ by $9.7\cdot10^{-17}$.

The nodal and finite element curves start from different global averages, for
the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:formulation}. No amplitude rescaling is
applied to hide this difference, since it is a property of the two models and
not an error of either.

\subsection{Regional vulnerability}

The network enters the model only through $L$, so it is natural to ask which
regions are the most dangerous places for the disease to start. Following the
infection-time experiment of \cite{fornari2019} we seed each of the $83$
regions in turn and record the time at which the network biomarker reaches
$95\%$. The reference performs this study with the Heterodimer model, so the
absolute times are not comparable and we compare only the ranking, which
depends on the network rather than on the kinetics.

At $\rho = 1$ the infection times span $0.02$ years, which is the same
homogenisation effect quantified above; at $\rho=0.02$ they span four years and
the ordering becomes meaningful. Three observations of the reference survive at
both scalings, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:vulnerability}.

The fastest seeds are subcortical hubs: we obtain the two caudate nuclei
followed by the two thalami, where the reference reports the putamen and the
insula. Both identify the same feature of the network, and the reference itself
notes that the caudate carries the largest number of connections in the graph.
The slowest seeds are the frontal and temporal poles; the frontal pole has the
smallest weighted degree of the entire graph, $2.29$. Finally, and most
interestingly, the entorhinal cortex, the region where the pathology is first
observed clinically and the seed used throughout this chapter, ranks $76$th and
$80$th of $83$ at $\rho=1$ and $78$th and $81$st at $\rho=0.02$: it is one of
the least efficient possible starting points. The reference draws from the same
observation the argument that this is part of why the early stages of the
disease are so hard to detect, and it is recovered here from an independent
reconstruction of the graph.

The third panel of the figure gives the mechanism. The infection time decreases
monotonically with the weighted degree of the seeded vertex. A poorly connected
seed keeps its concentration local, and the quadratic term of the reaction then
slows the growth of the network average.

\subsection{Deterministic model with regional reaction rates}
\label{sec:corti}

The last application follows \cite{corti2023}, who solve a patient-specific
stochastic Fisher--Kolmogorov model on a $246$-node graph and calibrate the
reaction coefficient region by region against two PET acquisitions. Neither the
patient graph nor the PET images are available to us, so we reproduce the
mathematical model, the time discretization and the estimated mean parameters,
and we state clearly that we do not reproduce the clinical trajectory of their
patient.

The reaction coefficient is piecewise constant over seven anatomical groups and
takes the mean values estimated in the reference, from $0.1801$ in the frontal
lobe to $0.0545$ in the occipital lobe; the nodal values are interpolated
linearly along each connection. The diffusivity is the normalised connectivity
$D_e = w_e/\max_e w_e$. The time discretization is the semi-implicit
Crank--Nicolson scheme of the reference: diffusion and the first concentration
factor are centred in time, and only the saturation factor is extrapolated,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C}^\star = \tfrac{3}{2}\mathbf{C}^{n} - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{C}^{n-1},
\qquad
G(\mathbf{C}^\star)_{ij}
  = \int_\Gamma \alpha\,[1-c^\star]\,\varphi_i\varphi_j ,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\Big(M + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2} K_D - \tfrac{\Delta t}{2} G(\mathbf{C}^\star)\Big)
  \mathbf{C}^{n+1}
= \Big(M - \tfrac{\Delta t}{2} K_D + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2} G(\mathbf{C}^\star)\Big)
  \mathbf{C}^{n}.
\end{equation}
Since $G$ changes at every step, the sparse time-step matrix is rebuilt and
factorised at every step, a point we return to in the performance analysis. We
use $\Delta t = 0.2$ years, four elements per connection, $3473$ degrees of
freedom and a horizon of $20$ years, and we replace the unavailable PET
projection by a documented synthetic datum: entorhinal and hippocampal regions
start at $c = 0.10$ and all others at $c = 0.01$.

The solution stays in $[0.0250, 0.6536]$ throughout, every regional average
grows monotonically, and the mean concentration sampled at the $83$ anatomical
vertices rises from $0.0143$ to $0.1578$. The absolute levels are far below
those of the reference, as they must be given the different initial datum, but
the ordering of the regions can be compared, and Table~\ref{tab:corti} does so.
Twenty of the twenty-one pairwise orderings agree, a rank correlation of
$0.96$. The single inversion is frontal against limbic and is explained by the
synthetic seed, which places $c=0.10$ on entorhinal and hippocampal vertices,
all of which belong to the limbic group. Everything below the leading pair is
reproduced in the published order using nothing but the seven reaction
coefficients and the connectome topology.

analysis that closes this work. Timings are taken in a separate release build
with visualisation and diagnostic output disabled, thread counts pinned to one,
and reported as the median of five runs after one warm-up.

Two facts emerge from Table~\ref{tab:performance} and they set the agenda for
the remainder of the work. First, more than $99\%$ of the time is spent in the
time loop and almost none in assembly or input, so the target for optimisation
is the repeated sparse factorisation forced by the semi-implicit scheme, not
the finite element assembly. Second, the cost per step is not a function of the
number of unknowns alone: the two-element mesh, with $1213$ unknowns, costs
exactly as much as the four-element mesh with $3473$. The one-element mesh has
a hub-dominated sparsity pattern inherited from the connectome, and sparse
factorisation depends on the elimination ordering and the resulting fill-in at
least as much as on the matrix dimension. This is precisely the observation
that motivates the reordering study of the next chapter, where bandwidth,
profile, factor fill and factorisation time are measured for different
orderings before any parallel scaling result is interpreted.
