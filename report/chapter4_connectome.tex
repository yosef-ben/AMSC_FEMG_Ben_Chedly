\section{Spreading of neurodegenerative diseases within the brain's connectome}

We have validated our finite element library for the solution of graph-based differential problems on a sequence of elliptic and parabolic benchmarks. We now consider a more application-oriented test case. In particular, we study the Fisher–Kolmogorov equation on a brain connectome's topology. This problem provides a natural extension of the previous linear parabolic models, as it combines diffusion along the graph with a nonlinear reaction term. At the same time, it provides a meaningful test for assessing the scalability of our framework through graph reordering and parallel programming techniques, with the particularly interesting perspective of modeling the propagation of infectious misfolded proteins through the brain, a process that is responsible for neurodegenerative diseases. The goal of this section is therefore not to provide a detailed biological study, but to investigate how the developed solver can be extended from controlled benchmark problems to a realistic network-based diffusion-reaction model.

\subsection{One-dimensional sensitivity analysis}
\label{sec:onedim}

Prion diseases are characterized by a chain reaction in which misfolded proteins force native ones into a similar pathogenic structure. A physics-based reaction-diffusion model can explain the spreading and growth of these proteins in neurodegenerative diseases: the Fisher-Kolmogorov equation combined with an anisotropic diffusion model \cite{weickenmeier2019prion,cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs,fornari2019prion}. Following the work described in \cite{weickenmeier2019prion}, we demonstrate the role of the individual model parameters through a one-dimensional sensitivity test, that we exploit also to validate the nonlinear solver before it touches a connectome. We solve
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} - d\,\frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2}
  - \alpha\,c\,(1-c) = 0,
\qquad (x,t) \in (-1,1) \times (0,20],
\label{eq:onedim}
\end{equation}
with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions,
\[
\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}(-1,t) = \frac{\partial c}{\partial x}(1,t) = 0 .
\]
The initial concentration is zero at every mesh node except at the central node, where $c(0,0)=0.1$. The interval is discretized using 200 linear finite elements. Backward Euler is employed with $\Delta t = 0.1$ and the nonlinear problem arising at each time step is solved by Newton's method; this is the fully implicit scheme of Section~\ref{sec:formulation}. The sensitivity analysis combines the diffusion coefficients $d \in \{1.0, 2.0, 4.0\}\cdot10^{-4}$ with the reaction coefficients $\alpha \in \{1.0,2.0,4.0\}$. These are the choices made in~\cite{weickenmeier2019prion}, including the number of elements and the number of time steps.
\noindent
Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity} presents the results using an activation-time representation. For every pair $(x,c)$, the color denotes the first time at which the concentration at position $x$ reaches a prescribed level of $c$. The black curves are therefore contours of equal activation time.
Increasing $\alpha$ accelerates the local conversion of healthy proteins, whereas increasing $d$ enhances their spatial propagation. Consequently, both parameters increase the propagation speed, although through different mechanisms. The results are in agreement with the ones in \cite{weickenmeier2019prion}.

\noindent
From this sensitivity test we can also derive the main properties of prion-like diseases:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the progression of the disease is inevitable if there is an initial infectious seeding;
    \item the critical amount of misfolded proteins (quantity that defines the end of the incubation period) is reached at the seeding site as soon as the initial concentration and growth rate $\alpha$ are higher;
    \item the disease progression is characterized by a local increase at the initial seeding region and a subsequent global spread across the domain;
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The reference reports a preliminary study in which the time step was varied from $\Delta t/4$ to $4\Delta t$, using $\Delta t = 0.1$ as the reference value, with $\alpha = 2.0$ and $d = 2\cdot10^{-4}$. The authors concluded that $\Delta t = 0.1$ was sufficiently small to obtain a converged solution, whereas larger time steps produced a spurious increase in spreading. That study is described in words only: neither the data nor a figure is given, so the used criterion behind the statement cannot be reconstructed. What we can do is to repeat the experiment in order to observe the spurious solutions obtained for larger time steps.\\
We resort to the coordinate $x_{f}$ of the right front of the solutions as a diagnostic reference, defined as the point where the profile of the curve crosses $c = 0.5$. All the runs are compared at the common time $T = 19.2$, the largest time that is a whole multiple of every step tested: $\Delta t = \{0.025, 0.05, 0.1,0.2, 0.3,0.4\}$. Fig.~\ref{fig:timestep} shows the outcome of the experiment. The qualitative observation of the reference is confirmed. Increasing the time step moves the front outward and at a certain point, the front disappears altogether: for $\Delta t \ge 0.2$ the interval is essentially full, so no $c = 0.5$ crossing exists at all. Large time steps do produce a spurious increase in numerical propagation, exactly as described.\\
Quantitatively, the reading is more delicate. The three time steps that admit a front give:
\[
x_{f} = \{0.6889, \quad 0.7268, \quad 0.8145\}
\qquad\text{for}\qquad
\Delta t = \{0.025,\ 0.05,\ 0.1\},
\]
so the solution at $\Delta t = 0.1$ is not yet insensitive to further temporal refinement. This does not directly contradict the reference, since the criterion used there to assess convergence is not reported. Our numerical result only shows that, when the front position is used as a diagnostic, the solution still changes appreciably under further temporal refinement. Panel (b) quantifies the same experiment with three dimensionless differences from the finest run ($\Delta t = 0.025)$, all evaluated on the profiles at the common time $T$.\\
The things this experiment does not establish should be stated plainly: it is not a study of the temporal order of convergence: the run at $\Delta t = 0.025$ is a numerical reference and not an exact solution, so the dimensionless plotted quantities are differences between two numerical solutions and not errors. The formal temporal orders of the two integrators, recorded in Table~\ref{tab:integrators}, are verified against a problem with an exact solution in the benchmark record.

\subsection{The brain connectome as a metric graph}
\label{sec:connectome}

Having verified the nonlinear solver on a controlled one-dimensional problem, we now move to the network on which the disease is assumed to spread, reflecting the prion-like hypothesis that misfolded proteins propagate through the brain's axonal fiber tracts. Following \cite{fornari2019prion}, we represent the brain as a weighted undirected graph $\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})$ whose vertices are anatomical regions and whose edges are the axonal bundles connecting them. The graph is extracted from the public Budapest Reference Connectome v3.0 \cite{szalkai2017budapest}, which aggregates the tractography of $418$ healthy subjects of the Human Connectome Project. The downloaded fine-resolution graph contains $1015$ parcels and $71604$ connections. For each fine connection, the file records how many subjects exhibit that connection, together with aggregate statistics about its fiber count and length. As a first preprocessing step, we retain only fine connections occurring in at least five subjects. This reduces the number of edges from $71604$ to $37477$, while leaving all $1015$ parcels in the graph. The five-subject threshold is not explicitly stated in~\cite{fornari2019prion}; rather, it is inferred from the public dataset as the unique integer threshold reproducing the $37477$ fine edges reported in the reference. For comparison, thresholds of four and six subjects yield $40895$ and $34718$ edges, respectively.\\
The resulting fine graph is then mapped onto the $83$ anatomical regions of the FreeSurfer parcellation, as in~\cite{fornari2019prion}. Several fine parcels therefore collapse onto the same anatomical region. Among the $37477$ retained fine connections, $7895$ connect parcels belonging to the same final region and consequently become internal to an aggregated node. The remaining $29582$ connections join distinct regions, but many connect different fine parcels
belonging to the same pair of anatomical regions. Such parallel connections are merged, resulting in the final graph with $83$ vertices and $1130$ edges.\\
Each retained fine connection $f$ is assigned the weight $w_f =
\frac{n_f}{<\ell_f>}$, where $n_f$ and $<\ell_f>$ \footnote{<($\cdot$)> identifies an average over the fibers belonging to the same fine connection.} denote its statistically aggregated values of fiber count and length, respectively. When several fine connections join the same pair of anatomical regions $i$ and $j$, their contributions are summed:
\[
A_{ij}
=
\sum_{f\in\mathcal{E}_{ij}}
\frac{n_f}{<\ell_f>}.
\]
Thus, an edge of the $83$-region graph generally represents the aggregate contribution of several finer parcel-to-parcel connections, i.e. a bundle. Before any simulation is performed, the reconstructed connectome is verified against the graph statistics reported in~\cite{fornari2019prion}. The topology is recovered exactly and the weighted statistics agree with the published values. Our results are reported in Table \ref{tab:connectome}\footnote{An important point concerns the provenance of the quantities related to the fibers. Although \cite{fornari2019prion} describes the edge weights in terms of fiber quantities averaged over the cohort, the published graph statistics are reproduced by using the \emph{median} fiber-count and length fields of the public dataset. Specifically, the reconstruction uses $w_f = \frac{n_f^{\mathrm{median}}} {(<\ell_f>)^{\mathrm{median}}}$, followed by summation over parallel fine connections. In contrast, the corresponding mean fields do not reproduce the published values. We therefore
retain the median-based reconstruction, while treating this choice as a data-driven reconstruction of the published graph rather than as a statement about the undocumented implementation used in the reference.}. The graph the solvers read is this one and not a variant of it: the finite element meshes list the $1130$ connections in the order of the weight file with unit metric length, and at one element per connection the assembled diffusion matrix reproduces the graph Laplacian $L = D - A$ built from the same weights entry by entry, the check being printed with every run.

\noindent
The definition of the connectivity weight has an important consequence for the resulting network. Since each fine-edge contribution is proportional to fibre count and inversely proportional to fibre length, connections supported by many short fibres receive larger weights, whereas long-range connections are penalized. After aggregation, this effect is inherited by the regional adjacency $A_{ij}$ through the sum of all fine connections joining the same pair of regions. At the level of the anatomical groups used later in this chapter, the total connectivity between the frontal and parietal groups is $174.8$ and that between the parietal and occipital groups is $189.7$. In contrast, the total frontal--temporal connectivity is only $0.85$, distributed over $14$ region-to-region connections, none of which exceeds $0.19$. The weighted network is therefore dominated by relatively strong short- and intermediate-range connections rather than by long-range bundles. Fig. \ref{fig:views}, \ref{fig:regions} and \ref{fig:connectogram} show the resulting computational domain.\\

\noindent
Fig.~\ref{fig:topology} summarizes the connectivity of the reconstructed
graph through the degree of its vertices in the non-weighted skeleton, their degree in the weighted graph and the (weighted) adjacency matrix. The number of connections per region varies between $6$ and $48$, consistently with~\cite{fornari2019prion}, which identifies the frontal pole, at the anterior tip of the hemisphere and the caudate, one of the deep subcortical nuclei, among the least and most connected regions, respectively. Weighting substantially changes this picture. The weighted degree spans $2.05 \le D_{ii} \le 127.64$, with a mean of $42.75$, in agreement with the values reported in the reference. The minimum occurs at the frontal pole and the maximum at the precentral gyrus, on the upper surface of the hemisphere, showing that a region with many connections does not necessarily have the largest total connectivity weight.\\
The adjacency matrix in panel~(c) reflects the same organization described in~\cite{fornari2019prion}. The two dense diagonal blocks correspond to connections within the two hemispheres, whereas the off-diagonal blocks are much sparser and contain the interhemispheric connections. There are strong connections also within
the four lobes, indicated through the eight red regions (dashed boxes) along the diagonal of the matrix (4 lobes $\times$ 2 hemispheres). The coloured strips along the four sides identify the seven anatomical groups used later in the analysis. The non-zero adjacency values span $0.0085 \le A_{ij} \le 35.3221$, with a mean of $1.5702$, reproducing the published range and mean to the reported precision. The most important feature of panel~(c) is not the identity of a single strong or weak connection, but the pronounced heterogeneity of the network: most pairs of regions are either disconnected or only weakly connected, whereas a comparatively small number of connections carry much larger weights. Together with the heterogeneous weighted degree in panel~(b), this reveals the presence of strongly connected hubs embedded in a much more weakly connected network. This structure is directly relevant to disease spreading. In the network model of~\cite{fornari2019prion}, highly connected regions are expected
to become effective hubs for the propagation of misfolded proteins: the disease does not propagate over a uniform network, but through a small number of preferentially connected pathways and hubs.

\subsection{Network model and metric-graph formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

We solve the Fisher--Kolmogorov equation posed on the (metric) graph presented in the previous section;  on every edge $e$:
\begin{equation}
\partial_t c(s,t) - \partial_s\!\left[D_e\,\partial_s c(s,t)\right]
  = \alpha(s)\,c(s,t)\,[1-c(s,t)].
\label{eq:fk}
\end{equation}
Up to the nonlinear right-hand side, this is the same parabolic problem studied in the first part of this work. The only new ingredient is the nonlinear reaction term. The references solve a simpler system, with one unknown per node and no spatial structure inside the connections. We will refer to the models of the references \cite{cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs, fornari2019prion} as the \textit{nodal models}, that serve as the term of comparison next to our finite element solution. Each reproduced experiment uses the time scheme and way of treating the nonlinear term of its own reference. Table~\ref{tab:integrators} summarizes the two schemes.

\subsection{Comparison with the network model}
\label{sec:comparison}

We reproduce the baseline simulation of~\cite{fornari2019prion}: conversion rate $\alpha=0.5$, misfolded protein seeded in the entorhinal cortex at $c_0=0.1$, all other regions healthy and $100$ implicit steps of $\Delta t = 0.4$ years. Following the reference, the evolution is summarized by a biomarker: for a group of regions $\mathcal{I}$, the abnormality is the concentration averaged
over its vertices and expressed as a percentage,
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathcal{I}}(t)
  = \frac{100}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{I \in \mathcal{I}} c_I(t),
\label{eq:biomarker}
\end{equation}
evaluated for the four cortical lobes and for the network as a whole. The biomarker curves have the expected sigmoid shape. The $50$-percent crossing of the network average occurs at $11.11$ years for the nodal model and at $12.68$ years for the finite element one; the difference is the metric-support effect described in Section~\ref{sec:reproduction}. The activation sequence, however, is not reproduced. \cite{fornari2019prion} report
a separation of roughly five years between the temporal and the occipital lobe; our four lobe curves cross the $50$-percent level within $3.9\cdot10^{-7}$ years of each other in the nodal model and $8.4\cdot10^{-3}$ years in the finite element model. Section~\ref{sec:damkohler} is devoted to explaining this discrepancy.

\subsubsection{Limits of an exact reproduction}
\label{sec:reproduction}

Before explaining the discrepancy it is worth stating precisely what can and
what cannot be claimed about it. First, the graph. Section~\ref{sec:connectome}
shows that the reconstructed connectome reproduces the topology and the
weighted statistics published by the reference, degree, adjacency and
weighted degree with their means, ranges and the regions attaining the
extremes, and that the operator handed to both solvers is the Laplacian of
that graph. A gross difference in the reconstruction of the connectome is
therefore an unlikely explanation for the different lobe dynamics. This does
not allow us to state that our adjacency matrix coincides entry by entry
with the one the authors used: the $83 \times 83$ matrix and the code of the
reference are not available, and the published statistics constrain the
matrix without determining it. Second, the discretization. The gap between
the two crossing times above, $11.11$ against $12.68$ years, is a difference
between the two discrete systems and not an error of either: in the nodal
model a value prescribed at a vertex is a point value, whereas in the finite
element system the consistent mass matrix gives it a metric support over the
incident connections and the reaction is integrated along them. Absolute
crossing times are moreover sensitive to the time step at the level of a
year, halving $\Delta t$ repeatedly moving the network crossing from $11.50$
years at $\Delta t = 0.8$ to $13.71$ years at $\Delta t = 0.05$, in our
simulation as in the reference, which uses the same step with an implicit
scheme; absolute times are therefore not compared between the two works.
The refinement study behind these numbers is kept in the benchmark record:
the connectome problem has no exact solution, so it measures the
sensitivity of the results to the discretization rather than proving
convergence.

Third, what the published description allows us to reconstruct. The
reference specifies the dataset and the parcellation, the edge weight as the
ratio of fibre number to fibre length, the operator $L = D - A$ with
$D_{II} = \sum_{J} A_{IJ}$, the conversion rate $\alpha = 0.5$, the seed at
$c_0 = 0.1$ in the entorhinal cortex, an implicit time integration with
$100$ steps of $\Delta t = 0.4$ years, and the biomarker as the sum of the
nodal concentrations. It does not specify which implicit scheme is used; how
the $1015$-node graph is aggregated to $83$ regions, in particular how
parallel and intra-region connections are treated and whether mean or
median fibre fields enter the weights, Section~\ref{sec:connectome}
recording that the printed statistics are reproduced by the median fields;
which of the $83$ regions form each lobe; the normalization that turns the
summed biomarker into the percentage of its figure; and the effective scale
of the transport term relative to the time scale of the dynamics. On this
last point the paper is not fully explicit: the adjacency is defined as a
fibre count divided by a fibre length in millimetres, while the dynamics is
expressed in years, so the relation between the structural weights and the
time unit is a possible point of ambiguity, which we resolve by the most
literal reading, taking the weights as they are. Where a choice was needed
we made the most literal one throughout, and the resulting graph reproduces
every published statistic at printed precision.

With the model and the parameters reconstructed in this way, the Laplacian
built from the weights $n_{IJ}/l_{IJ}$, $\alpha = 0.5$, the entorhinal seed
and the declared time discretization, we do not recover the marked
inter-lobe separation shown by the reference. Two alternative explanations
are excluded or strongly weakened by tests already at hand. The
near-synchronization is not a consequence of how the regions are grouped
into lobes: at this scale all $83$ regional curves cross the $50$-percent
level within $6.4\cdot10^{-5}$ years of each other in the nodal model and
within $0.20$ years in the finite element one, so no assignment of regions
to lobes can separate the lobe curves by years. Nor is it recovered by
reasonable changes of the discretization: with the semi-implicit scheme in
place of the fully implicit one, with the time step halved four times from
$0.8$ to $0.05$ years and with eight elements per connection, the four lobe
curves cross within $0.04$ years of each other in every combination
(benchmark record), the changes moving the absolute times but not the
separation. Section~\ref{sec:damkohler} shows which quantity the separation
does respond to.

\subsection{What controls the regional separation}
\label{sec:damkohler}

Section~\ref{sec:comparison} ended on a disagreement: the reference reports
lobes activating years apart, while our curves coincide. This section shows
that one dimensionless number controls the separation.

Consider the nodal model with the connectivity weights scaled by a uniform
factor $\rho > 0$, so that its graph Laplacian $L = D - A$ becomes $\rho L$
and $\rho = 1$ recovers the published model; in the metric-graph
formulation the same factor multiplies every edge diffusivity $D_e$. Two rates then compete.
Linearised around a small concentration, the reaction grows every regional
value at the rate $\alpha$. Diffusion damps every contrast between regions;
expanding on the eigenvectors of $L$, the slowest, longest-lived contrast
decays at the rate $\rho\lambda_2$, where $\lambda_2$ is the smallest
non-zero eigenvalue of $L$ (its Fiedler value). The reaction therefore
differentiates the regions on the time scale $1/\alpha$, while the network
erases their differences on the time scale $1/(\rho\lambda_2)$. The
Damk\"ohler number is the ratio of the two,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Da}
  \;=\; \frac{1/(\rho\lambda_2)}{1/\alpha}
  \;=\; \frac{\alpha}{\rho\,\lambda_2},
\label{eq:damkohler}
\end{equation}
the homogenization time of the slowest contrast over the reaction time.
When $\mathrm{Da} \ll 1$ the graph homogenizes before the reaction can tell
the regions apart, and coincident regional curves are the correct outcome;
when $\mathrm{Da} \gg 1$ the contrasts imprinted by the topology and by the
seed survive up to saturation, and the curves separate. For the
reconstructed connectome, $\lambda_2 = 0.7723$, computed from the same edge
weights the solver uses.

At the parameters as we reconstruct them, $\rho = 1$ and $\alpha = 0.5$, we
obtain $\mathrm{Da} = 0.65$. The connectome homogenizes faster than the
reaction develops, and coincident lobe curves are the correct solution of
the nodal model at that scale, not a numerical artefact.
Figure~\ref{fig:damkohler} quantifies this by sweeping $\rho$: the lobe
separation grows from $3.9\cdot10^{-7}$ years at $\mathrm{Da}=0.65$ to $6.99$
years at $\mathrm{Da}=647$, seven decades of variation with the topology, the
seed and the conversion rate held fixed. Weakening the network transport
progressively restores a multi-year separation between the lobes, and a
separation comparable to the one shown in~\cite{fornari2019prion} is
reached at $\mathrm{Da}$ of order $10^{2}$, that is at $\rho$ close to
$0.005$, about two hundred times weaker than the literal scale.

The result must be read for what it is. The sensitivity analysis shows that
the inter-lobe separation is strongly controlled by the relative scale of
network transport and reaction, whereas Section~\ref{sec:reproduction}
shows that it does not respond to the grouping of the regions or to the
discretization; the sweep therefore identifies the mechanism controlling
the discrepancy. It does not establish which effective scaling was used in
the original implementation: the matrix and the code of the reference are
not available, and the relation between its structural weights and its time
unit is the one point its description leaves not fully explicit. Taken
together with the agreement of the reconstructed graph with every published
statistic, the discrepancy is most naturally located in this effective
scaling of the transport term rather than in the connectome itself, but we
do not claim more than that.

This also reconciles two results of this chapter that would otherwise look
contradictory. The deterministic model of Section~\ref{sec:corti} normalizes
the diffusivity as $D_e = w_e/\max_e w_e$, that is $\rho = 0.0283$, and uses
regional reaction rates whose average over the $83$ vertices is $0.1252$; its
Damk\"ohler number is $5.73$, on the other side of the crossover, which is why
its regional curves separate clearly while those of
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} do not.

\subsubsection{A validity boundary for the discretization}
\label{sec:boundary}

The same sweep bounds the finite element formulation, and the boundary
deserves a precise statement. The violation of the physical range grows with
$\mathrm{Da}$ well before the failure: the transient undershoot of the
finite element solution is $-1.3\cdot10^{-4}$ at $\mathrm{Da}=0.65$ and
already $[-0.17, 1.04]$ at $\mathrm{Da}=12.9$, from which the solution still
recovers and converges to the healthy-to-infected plateau. Above
$\mathrm{Da}$ of about $13$, that is $\rho \le 0.02$ at $\alpha=0.5$, it no
longer recovers and diverges: at $\rho = 0.005$ the nodal reference stays
within $[0.99985, 1]$ while the finite element solution reaches
$[-507, 523]$. The boundary therefore separates recoverable from
unrecoverable violations of the maximum principle, not violation from
compliance.
The failure occurs identically with the fully implicit and with the
semi-implicit scheme, so it is not a failure of the Newton solver. It
persists at $1$, $2$, $4$ and $8$ elements per connection, so it is not a
matter of resolving the front.

The cause is the consistent mass matrix. Its off-diagonal entries are positive,
so the semi-discrete system does not satisfy a discrete maximum principle;
while diffusion dominates, the diffusion matrix compensates, and once the
reaction dominates nothing does. The nodal model and the Hadamard product used
in~\cite{cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs} carry a diagonal mass with the reaction
evaluated at the vertices, so no positive off-diagonal mass entry exists to
violate the maximum principle, which is why they remain bounded at every
scaling tested.
The defect is a property of the discretization, not of the connectome: on
the one-dimensional problem of Section~\ref{sec:onedim}, with the sharp
single-node datum at $\alpha=2$ and $d=10^{-4}$, refining the time step
makes the undershoot grow rather than shrink, from $-1.5\cdot10^{-13}$ at
$\Delta t = 0.1$ to $-9.1\cdot10^{-4}$ at $\Delta t = 0.025$. Introducing an
optional lumped mass matrix is therefore the natural way to extend the
present formulation into the reaction-dominated regime, and we leave it as
the immediate continuation of this work. Every finite element result of this
chapter sits at $\mathrm{Da} \le 5.73$, well inside the reliable region;
where a larger $\mathrm{Da}$ is required, in Sections~\ref{sec:staging}
and~\ref{sec:vulnerability}, the nodal system takes over, and the text says
so there.

\subsection{Seeding patterns and expected progression}
\label{sec:staging}

The two hallmark proteins of Alzheimer's disease enter the model through the
initial condition alone. Tau inclusions appear first in the transentorhinal
region, and the expected progression is the Braak sequence: entorhinal
cortex, then the temporal lobe, then the interconnected neocortex.
Amyloid-$\beta$ deposits appear first in the neocortex, and the expected
progression runs the other way, downwards, with the deep structures last
\cite{weickenmeier2019prion}. On the graph the two seedings are the entorhinal
vertices at $c_0=0.1$, the seed used throughout this chapter, and the $58$
vertices of the four cortical lobes at the same level.

Figure~\ref{fig:expected} shows the two computed progressions below their
clinical counterparts, the staging drawings of figure 1
of~\cite{weickenmeier2019prion}, in the construction of figure 1
of~\cite{fornari2019prion}: three stages, selected as the first instants at
which the network mean reaches $10$, $40$ and $80$ percent. The solution
shown is the nodal network model
at $\rho = 0.005$, the scaling at which Section~\ref{sec:damkohler} obtains
a separation of the magnitude the reference publishes; at this scaling the consistent-mass
finite element formulation leaves the physical range
(Section~\ref{sec:boundary}), while the nodal solution remains in $[0,1]$
throughout, and the figure script verifies it.

Both expected patterns are recovered. The tau row starts from a single dark
sphere at the entorhinal cortex, reaches the temporal neighbourhood first
and involves the whole network last, over $10.4$, $14.8$ and $18.8$ years.
The amyloid row starts from a faint uniform cortical mantle and descends:
averaging the first crossings of $c=0.5$ over each anatomical group, the
four seeded lobes activate between $4.5$ and $4.9$ years, the insula at
$5.6$, the limbic belt at $6.3$, and the subcortical nuclei last at $7.7$
years, three years behind the cortex, which is the downward sequence the
literature describes. The one caveat carries over from
Section~\ref{sec:damkohler}: within the late tau stages the ordering of the
remaining lobes reflects the connectivity of the graph and not the clinical
sequence. Behind the staged renders the four lobes cross the $50$-percent
level in the order temporal, occipital, parietal, frontal, the same order
as in Figure~\ref{fig:damkohler}; the reference reports temporal, frontal,
parietal, occipital. In our simulations the frontal group is last because
of the weak connectivity of the frontal pole, the least connected region of
the graph, a property the reference also reports; whether the same holds
for the matrix used there cannot be checked for the reasons given in
Section~\ref{sec:reproduction}. With a uniform conversion rate,
connectivity is the only anatomy our model carries, so this is the order it
produces on this graph. The clinical order emerges once the biology that
carries it enters the coefficients: with the regional rates of
Section~\ref{sec:corti}, calibrated by that reference on patient data, the
frontal group is among the first and the occipital group is last
(Table~\ref{tab:corti} and Figure~\ref{fig:activationorder}), and
Section~\ref{sec:corti} shows the recruitment to be set by those rates
rather than by propagation.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the conversion rate}
\label{sec:alpha}

We repeat the conversion-rate experiment of~\cite{fornari2019prion} with
$\alpha = 0, 0.1, \dots, 0.5$ over $80$ years. In the reference the conversion
rate is not a free parameter but a combination of production, clearance and
misfolding rates, so lowering it is the model's representation of a therapeutic
intervention.

The qualitative conclusion of the reference is recovered: lowering $\alpha$
delays the transition but does not create an intermediate stable state, and
every positive conversion rate drives the network to $c=1$. The $50$-percent
crossing of the network average moves from $11.11$ years at $\alpha = 0.5$ to
$59.33$ years at $\alpha = 0.1$ in the nodal model, and from $12.68$ to $67.81$
years in the finite element model. For $\alpha = 0$ the reaction vanishes and
the two conserved quantities are preserved to round-off: the nodal average
drifts by $1.1\cdot10^{-14}$ and the normalized finite element metric integral
$\mathbf{1}^{\!\top} M \mathbf{C}/\mathbf{1}^{\!\top} M \mathbf{1}$ by
$8.0\cdot10^{-16}$.

The nodal and finite element curves start from different global averages, for
the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:reproduction}: a value prescribed at a
vertex acquires a metric support over the incident connections in the
finite element domain. No amplitude rescaling is
applied to hide this difference, since it is a property of the two models and
not an error of either.

\subsection{Regional vulnerability}
\label{sec:vulnerability}

The network enters the model only through $L$, so it is natural to ask which
regions are the most dangerous places for the disease to start. Following the
infection-time study of~\cite{fornari2019prion} we seed each of the $83$ regions in
turn and record the time at which the network biomarker reaches $95\%$. The
sweep runs on the nodal model, because one of its two
scalings lies beyond the validity boundary of Section~\ref{sec:boundary}. The
reference performs this experiment with the Heterodimer model rather than with
Fisher--Kolmogorov, so the absolute times are not comparable and we compare only
the ranking, which depends on the network rather than on the kinetics.

At $\rho = 1$ the infection times span $0.02$ years, which is the homogenization
effect quantified in Section~\ref{sec:damkohler}; at $\rho=0.02$ they span four
years and the ordering becomes measurable. Three observations of the reference
survive at both scalings.

The fastest seeds are subcortical hubs: we obtain the two caudate nuclei
followed by the two thalami, where the reference reports the putamen and the
insula. Both identify the same feature of the network, and the reference itself
notes that the caudate carries the largest number of connections in the graph.
The slowest seeds are the frontal and temporal poles; the frontal pole has the
smallest weighted degree of the entire graph, $2.05$. Finally, and most
interestingly, the entorhinal cortex, the region where the pathology is first
observed clinically and the seed used throughout this chapter, ranks $76$th and
$80$th of $83$ at $\rho=1$ and $78$th and $81$st at $\rho=0.02$: it is one of
the least efficient possible starting points. The reference draws from the same
observation the argument that this is part of why the early stages of the
disease are so hard to detect, and it is recovered here from an independent
reconstruction of the graph.

The third panel of the figure gives the mechanism. The infection time decreases
monotonically with the weighted degree of the seeded vertex: a poorly connected
seed keeps its concentration local, and the quadratic term of the reaction then
slows the growth of the network average.

\caption{Regional vulnerability, after figure 9 of~\cite{fornari2019prion}. Each of

and (b) the same quantity on the anatomy, both at $\rho=0.02$; (c) infection

\end{figure}

\subsection{Deterministic model with regional reaction rates}
\label{sec:corti}

The last application follows~\cite{cortiFisherKolmogorovGraphs}, who solve a patient-specific
stochastic Fisher--Kolmogorov model on a $246$-node Brainnetome graph and
calibrate the reaction coefficient region by region against two PET
acquisitions. Neither the patient graph nor the PET images are available to us,
so we reproduce the mathematical model, the time discretization and the
estimated mean parameters, and we state clearly that we do not reproduce the
clinical trajectory of their patient.

The reaction coefficient is piecewise constant over seven anatomical groups and
takes the mean values estimated in table 1 of the reference: $0.1801$ in the
frontal lobe, $0.1421$ temporal, $0.0627$ parietal, $0.1005$ insular, $0.1351$
limbic, $0.0545$ occipital and $0.1147$ in the subcortical nuclei. The nodal
values are interpolated linearly along each connection. The diffusivity is the
normalized connectivity $D_e = w_e/\max_e w_e$; the reference does not state a
normalization, and Section~\ref{sec:damkohler} shows that this choice is what
places the simulation in the regime where regional curves separate.

The time discretization is the semi-implicit scheme
of Table~\ref{tab:integrators}, which is the one the reference adopts. We use
$\Delta t = 0.2$ years, four elements per connection, $3473$ degrees of freedom
and a horizon of $20$ years, and we replace the unavailable PET projection by a documented
synthetic datum: entorhinal and hippocampal regions start at $c = 0.10$ and all
others at $c = 0.01$.

The solution stays in $[0.0250, 0.6536]$ throughout, every regional average
grows monotonically, and the mean concentration sampled at the $83$ anatomical
vertices rises from $0.0143$ to $0.1578$. The absolute levels are far below
those of the reference, as they must be given the different initial datum, but
the ordering of the regions can be compared, and Table~\ref{tab:corti} does so.
Twenty of the twenty-one pairwise orderings agree, a rank correlation of
$0.96$. The single inversion is frontal against limbic and is explained by the
initial condition: the synthetic seed places $c=0.10$ on entorhinal and
hippocampal vertices, all of which belong to the limbic group. Everything below
the leading pair is reproduced in the published order using nothing but the
seven reaction coefficients and the connectome topology.

The same run, refined to eight elements per connection and extended to $60$
years, settles how the network is recruited. Colouring each of the
$10\,170$ degrees of freedom with the first year its concentration reaches
$0.5$ gives an anterior-to-posterior gradient whose obvious reading, a front
travelling outward from the seed, is wrong: over the region vertices the
activation time correlates with the local reaction coefficient at
$r=-0.841$ and with the graph distance from the seed at $r=+0.065$, and the
graph eccentricity from the seed is $2$, every region lying within two
connections of the entorhinal and hippocampal seed. The recruitment order is
set by the regional conversion rates, $0.1801$ in the frontal group against
$0.0545$ in the occipital one, not by propagation, which is the conclusion
of Section~\ref{sec:damkohler} reached independently.

The role of the calibrated rates is then isolated by a control experiment,
in the construction of the infection-time figure of~\cite{fornari2019prion}.
The same refined simulation is repeated with the seven regional rates
replaced by their vertex mean, $0.1252$: the reaction budget and the
Damk\"ohler number are unchanged, and the only anatomy left in the model is
the connectivity. Figure~\ref{fig:activationorder} colours every vertex by
its activation time, the first stored year at which the concentration
reaches $0.5$, for the two variants. With the calibrated rates the mean
group activation runs from $30.2$ years in the frontal group, first as in
table 3 of the reference, to $43.0$ years in the occipital group, last by
four clear years, which is the clinical tail of the staging. With the
uniform rate the picture inverts into the connectivity's order: the total
spread collapses from $12.9$ to $3.0$ years, the occipital group moves up
to $35.0$ years, and the frontal group, dragged by its weakly connected
pole, activates last at $36.2$ years. On this connectome the clinical order
of the groups is therefore carried by the regional biology and not by the
network, which is the conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:damkohler} reached
from the opposite side. The staged concentration sections of this run
remain in the benchmark record.

\subsection{Sequential baseline}
\label{sec:performance}

The deterministic model above is also the reference problem for the performance
analysis that opens the next chapter. Timings are taken in a separate release
build with visualisation and diagnostic output disabled, thread counts pinned
to one, and reported as the median of five runs after one warm-up.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\hline
Elements per connection & Unknowns & Matrix nonzeros & $100$ steps [s] &
Per step [s] \\
\hline
$1$ & $83$   & $2343$  & $0.0832$ & $0.00083$ \\
$2$ & $1213$ & $5733$  & $1.8694$ & $0.01869$ \\
$4$ & $3473$ & $12513$ & $1.5486$ & $0.01549$ \\
$8$ & $7993$ & $26073$ & $2.2343$ & $0.02234$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Sequential baseline for the deterministic model. Assembly accounts for
less than one percent of the total in every refined case.}
\label{tab:performance}
\end{table}

Two facts emerge from Table~\ref{tab:performance} and they set the agenda for
what follows. First, more than $99\%$ of the time is spent in the time loop and
almost none in assembly or input, so the target for optimisation is the
repeated sparse factorisation forced by the semi-implicit scheme
(Table~\ref{tab:integrators}), not the finite element assembly. Second, the cost per step is
not a function of the number of unknowns alone: the two-element mesh, with
$1213$ unknowns, costs more than the four-element mesh with $3473$.
The one-element mesh has a hub-dominated sparsity pattern inherited from the
connectome, and sparse factorisation depends on the elimination ordering and
the resulting fill-in at least as much as on the matrix dimension.

For scale, \cite{fornari2019prion} report that their nodal model runs its $100$
implicit steps in $0.55$ s without output on a standard laptop; our
metric-graph formulation at the same $83$ unknowns takes $0.083$ s for its
$100$ semi-implicit steps. The machines and the schemes differ, so this is an
order-of-magnitude check rather than a comparison, but it confirms that the P1
metric-graph formulation carries no cost penalty over the nodal network model
at the coarsest mesh, while allowing arbitrary refinement inside the
connections.

This is precisely the observation that motivates the reordering study of the
next chapter, where bandwidth, profile, factor fill and factorisation time are
measured for different orderings before any parallel scaling result is
interpreted.
